### 1.4 *R.G. Anand v. M/s Delux Films & Ors.*

- **Citation:** (1978) 4 SCC 118; AIR 1978 SC 1613; 1979 SCR (1) 218
- **Court and date:** Supreme Court of India, 18 August 1978. Bench: S. Murtaza Fazal Ali, Jaswant Singh and R.S. Pathak, JJ. Civil Appeal No. 2030 of 1968.
- **Source:** official Supreme Court judgment archive, https://api.sci.gov.in/jonew/judis/5032.pdf
- **Status:** **VERIFIED.** I inspected the passage summarising the propositions (numbered 1 to 7) at reporter pages 248 to 250 of the archive copy. That copy prints reporter page numbers, not paragraph numbers, so no paragraph numbers are given.
- **Propositions relied on (my words):**
  1. There is no copyright in an idea, subject matter, theme, plot or historical or legendary fact; protection attaches to the form in which they are expressed (proposition 1).
  2. A useful test, where the same idea is developed differently, is whether a reader, spectator or viewer gets an unmistakable impression that the later work is a copy of the original (proposition 3).
  3. Where the theme is the same but presented and treated differently, the later work becomes a new work and no violation arises (proposition 4).
  4. Broad dissimilarities negativing copying matter (proposition 5), and infringement must be proved by clear and cogent evidence (proposition 6).
- **Short quotations inspected:** proposition 1 includes the words "There can be no copyright in an idea, subject matter, themes, plots". Proposition 3 includes "gets an unmistakable impression that the subsequent work appears to be a copy of the original."
- **Caution:** the case concerned a stage play and a film. Its application to photographs is by analogy.
- **Subsequent treatment:** not checked systematically. Commentary records it as consistently followed, including in *Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak* and *Krishika Lulla v. Shyam Vithalrao Devkatta*, (2016) 2 SCC 521. I have not read those passages.

### 1.5 *The Cinema Resource Centre v. Ganga Rudraiah*

- **Court and date:** Madras High Court, Division Bench (P. Velmurugan and K. Govindarajan Thilakavadi, JJ.). O.S.A. (CAD) No. 17 of 2022 with C.M.P. No. 2260 of 2022, arising from C.S. No. 674 of 2017. Reserved 14 July 2026; pronounced 27 August 2026.
- **Source:** judgment text at https://indiankanoon.org/doc/141507651/ (unofficial database reproducing the judgment; paragraph numbers as printed there).
- **Status:** **VERIFIED** as to the passages inspected (paras 15 to 24). Because the text was read on an unofficial database rather than the High Court's own site, the citation should be confirmed against the official copy before use in any filing.
- **Propositions relied on (my words):**
  1. Under proviso (b) to s. 17, where a photograph is taken for valuable consideration at a person's instance, that person is the first owner unless there is an agreement to the contrary (para 19).
  2. The absence of a written agreement, of documentary proof of payment, and of evidence from the photographers was not fatal; the court weighed the oral evidence, admissions and surrounding circumstances (para 17).
  3. The court applied the civil standard of probability and relied in part on the fact that no one else, including the photographers, claimed copyright (paras 19 to 20).
  4. The appeal was dismissed and the trial court's decree confirmed (paras 23 to 24).
- **Short quotation inspected:** para 19 states that such a person "will be the first owner, unless there is an agreement to the contrary."
- **Caution and use in this project:** persuasive, not binding in Delhi. I cite it as a contrast: in this fictional scenario there *is* a written agreement to the contrary, which is the factor that was absent there.
- **Subsequent treatment:** not checked. The judgment is recent.

### 2.1 *Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak*
- **Citation:** (2008) 1 SCC 1; AIR 2008 SC 809. Supreme Court, 12 December 2007. Bench: B.N. Agrawal and P.P. Naolekar, JJ.
- **Source:** judgment text at https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1062099/ — I read the case details and paras 6 to 37 but could not reach the Court's own conclusions, which fall later in a long document.
- **Proposition relied on:** that Indian law requires skill and judgment with a minimal degree of creativity, rather than labour alone, for originality in a derivative work. The phrase commonly quoted from the judgment ("flavour of minimum requirement of creativity") appears in commentary; I did not see it in the text I read.
- **Why it still appears:** used only for a general standard that is not decisive for a staged photograph. **Do not quote this case without reading the concluding paragraphs.**
- **Subsequent treatment not checked.**

### 2.2 *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal*
- **Citation:** (2020) 7 SCC 1; 2020 SCC OnLine SC 571. Supreme Court, 14 July 2020. Civil Appeal Nos. 20825-20826 of 2017 and connected appeals.
- **Source:** the operative conclusion, reproduced at https://www.lawweb.in/2020/07/supreme-court-latest-judgment.html — an unofficial blog. I did not reach the official judgment. The conclusion there is lettered (a) to (d) with no paragraph numbers.
- **Propositions relied on:** *Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer*, (2014) 10 SCC 473, as clarified, is the law on s. 65B of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872; *Tomaso Bruno* is per incuriam and *Shafhi Mohammad* is overruled; a certificate is unnecessary where the original document itself is produced, which the owner of a device can do by stepping into the witness box; where the computer system cannot be brought to court, the only route is s. 65B(1) with a s. 65B(4) certificate.
- **Short quotation inspected:** the conclusion includes the words "Anvar P.V. (supra), as clarified by us hereinabove, is the law".
- **Caution:** the case concerns the repealed 1872 Act. How far its reasoning maps onto s. 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 is an open question that I did not resolve. The widely used description of the certificate as a "condition precedent" appears in commentary and in the blog's title, not in the conclusion text I read.
- **Subsequent treatment:** commentary records it as followed in later decisions; I did not read them.

### 2.4 *Yamini Manohar v. T.K.D. Keerthi*
- **Citation:** (2024) 5 SCC 815; also reported as 2023 SCC OnLine SC 1382. Supreme Court.
- **Source:** quoted by the Delhi High Court in a judgment on its own website (https://delhihighcourt.nic.in/app/showFileJudgment/SMP19032026SC6342023_210649.txt), which reproduces part of para 5, and in commentary.
- **Proposition relied on:** where a plaint seeks urgent interim relief, the commercial court should examine the nature and subject matter of the suit, the cause of action and the prayer; a prayer for urgent interim relief "should not be a disguise or mask to wriggle out of and get over" s. 12A.
- **Caution:** read only as quoted. The two reported citations should be reconciled against the official text.
- **Subsequent treatment:** commentary cites *Dhanbad Fuels (P) Ltd. v. Union of India*, (2025) 9 SCC 424 and *Novenco Building & Industry A/S v. Xero Energy Engineering Solutions (P) Ltd.* as applying it. I did not read either.

### 2.5 *Pune Bar Association v. Union of India & Ors.*
- **Citation:** Writ Petition (Civil) No. 599 of 2026. Supreme Court, order dated 22 May 2026. Coram: Surya Kant, C.J., Joymalya Bagchi and Vipul M. Pancholi, JJ. No neutral citation seen.
- **Source:** order text at https://www.caseciter.com/content/files/2026/05/Pune-Bar-Association-v.-Union-of-India---S.63-4--BSA---Hash-Value-Disclosure---Constitutional-Validity.pdf — an unofficial database reproducing the order. Paragraph numbers as printed there.
- **Propositions relied on:** the certificate requirement in s. 63(4), including disclosure of the hash value, "cannot be said to be either arbitrary or unreasonable so as to suffer from the vice of manifest arbitrariness" (para 4); reading ss. 39(1) and 39(2) BSA together, a person shown on unimpeachable material to have special skill in computer science and cyber forensics "may sign Part B of the Schedule as an expert", and is not limited to examiners notified under s. 79A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 (para 7); the contrary view of the Madras High Court is not to be treated as a binding precedent, and the question of law was kept open (para 7).
- **Caution:** this is an order declining to entertain the petition, not a full judgment on the merits. Counsel should assess how much weight it carries. The order does not address when Part B is required, nor the transitional position under s. 170.
- **Subsequent treatment not checked.**

### 2.6 Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, s. 170 and the Schedule
- **Status:** PARTIALLY VERIFIED. The India Code PDF is long and the parts I could read stopped before Chapter XII and the Schedule.
- **Propositions relied on, from reproductions on unofficial sites** (including https://www.advocatekhoj.com/library/bareacts/bharatiyaaakshya2023/170.php and https://www.advocatekhoj.com/library/bareacts/bharatiyaaakshya2023/b.php): s. 170(1) repeals the Indian Evidence Act, 1872; s. 170(2) saves any application, trial, inquiry, investigation, proceeding or appeal pending immediately before commencement, which continues under the 1872 Act. ### 2.7 Limitation Act, 1963, Schedule Article 88 and s. 22
- **Status:** PARTIALLY VERIFIED. Not read in an official consolidated text.
- **Article 88, as reproduced in the Law Commission's 89th Report (https://www.advocatekhoj.com/library/lawreports/limitationact1963/88.php):** "For compensation for infringing copyright or any other exclusive privilege. Three years. The date of the infringement."
- **Section 22, as quoted in a Delhi High Court judgment on the court's own site (https://delhihighcourt.nic.in/app/showFileJudgment/AJB14072025RFA5522025_133103.pdf):** in the case of a continuing breach of contract or a continuing tort, a fresh period of limitation begins to run at every moment during which the breach or tort continues.
- **Caution:** whether continued display of a web image, or continued sale of packaging, is a continuing tort or a series of separate wrongs is not settled by these sources. Commentary notes that s. 22 has been held not to apply to successive wrongs in some cases. Flagged for counsel.

### 2.8 Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and the DPDP Rules, 2025
- **Status:** PARTIALLY VERIFIED. Section 17(1)(a) ("the processing of personal data is necessary for enforcing any legal right or claim") was seen in reproductions on unofficial sites, not in the official gazette text I could reach.
- **Commencement, from commentary and a PIB document:** the DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified by G.S.R. 846(E) dated 13 November 2025 (a PIB release refers to 14 November 2025). Commencement is phased: rules 1, 2 and 17 to 21 immediately; consent-manager provisions after 12 months; the operative rules and most sections, including s. 17, from about 13 May 2027.
- **Use in this project:** only to note that enforcement-related processing is exempted from certain obligations, and that data minimisation is good practice regardless. The protocol does not depend on the exact commencement date.

### 2.9 Information Technology Act, 2000, s. 79 and the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, rule 3(1)(d)
- **Status:** PARTIALLY VERIFIED for the Rules; **the text of s. 79 was not inspected at all** and is described only through secondary sources.
- **Propositions from commentary** (including MeitY's press release at https://negd.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Press-Release_Press-Information-Bureau-3.pdf and law-firm notes): rule 3(1)(d) requires an intermediary to remove or disable access to unlawful information within 36 hours of actual knowledge; the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Amendment Rules, 2025, notified on 22 October 2025 and reported as effective from 15 November 2025, confine "actual knowledge" to a court order or a reasoned written intimation from an authorised officer not below a specified rank, with monthly review.
- **Caution:** used only in the optional platform-takedown step, which in practice runs through the platform's own policy process rather than this rule.

### 2.10 *MySpace Inc. v. Super Cassettes Industries Ltd.*
- **Citation:** Delhi High Court, Division Bench (S. Ravindra Bhat and Deepa Sharma, JJ.), 23 December 2016; reported at 236 (2017) DLT 478; FAO(OS) 540/2011.
- **Status:** PARTIALLY VERIFIED. I did not read the judgment. Propositions come from law-firm notes and reports: for internet intermediaries, s. 51(a)(ii) of the Copyright Act, 1957 requires actual knowledge of specific infringing content rather than general awareness; ss. 79 and 81 of the IT Act and s. 51(a)(ii) are to be read harmoniously; the rights holder was required to supply specific works with their locations (URLs), which the platform was to remove within 36 hours.
- **Caution:** the relationship between this decision and the 2025 amendment to rule 3(1)(d) was not researched. Do not cite without reading the judgment.

- **Status:** PARTIALLY VERIFIED. ## Note on research method

I began with official sources (India Code, the Copyright Office, the Supreme Court's judgment archive, the Delhi High Court's website) and used unofficial databases and law-firm commentary only to locate material or where the official text was not reachable. Several official PDFs are long enough that only part could be read in one pass; where that affected a provision, the limit is recorded above rather than filled in from recollection. No citation, paragraph number, quotation or legal test in this repository was invented.
